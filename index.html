The Aghi Lab studies how the brain-tumor microenvironment shapes treatment response. Its recent non-lytic viral immunotherapy study made me think about a practical question: when a tumor signal falls, how do we distinguish a treatment response from a change in which animals remain under observation?

I built a small review tool over the study’s public animal-level records. It keeps missing imaging measurements visible, tests how non-tumor endpoints affect interpretation, and turns those checks into a recording plan for the next cohort.

How it works

Figure 6 of Haddad et al. (2026) compares PBS or RRV-RLI, with vehicle or temozolomide (TMZ), in a mouse glioblastoma model. RLI is a receptor-linked IL-15 fusion protein; RRV-RLI is its retroviral delivery treatment. Each imaging trace is one anonymous source-column series; each endpoint record is one animal.

These are separate views, not matched animals: the public workbook does not provide an identifier linking the two sheets. All results below are descriptive reanalyses, not new experimental findings or treatment recommendations.

The composite is a sensitivity analysis, not an estimate of all-cause mortality. The workbook does not establish causes for every endpoint. Neither curve resolves informative censoring, and small groups do not support a treatment ranking by themselves. The 60-day default belongs to this exploratory tool, not a prespecified endpoint of the publication.

Why this matters for the lab

A useful next step would be to preserve a stable animal ID across imaging, endpoint adjudication and tissue measurements. If reduced bioluminescence agrees with independently assessed tumor burden while tolerability remains acceptable, confidence in the tumor response increases. If those measurements diverge, the priority becomes reconciling the assay and endpoint—not declaring a stronger response.

This is a proposed follow-up, not a claim about the lab’s internal plans. It would require matched controls, a prespecified imaging schedule, blinded measurement review and lab-approved endpoint definitions. Download a next-cohort recording schema; the current anonymous sheets cannot supply those missing links.

Methods note

Primary data: Haddad et al., Nature Communications (2026), Source Data workbook, Fig6B and Fig6C–G (CC BY 4.0). Derived values are this project’s reanalysis, not author-endorsed results. Day-3 normalization is within each BLI column. No imputation, model fitting, hypothesis-test claims or cross-sheet animal matching. Tail estimates can depend on very few animals.

Textual missing annotations are retained verbatim. BLI is an imaging signal, not a direct cell count; source data alone cannot verify ROI placement or acquisition settings. Kaplan–Meier estimates assume non-informative censoring; toxicity-related censoring challenges that interpretation.
